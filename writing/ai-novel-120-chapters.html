Method

How I finished a
573,623-character novel

A-Bullrager · October 2026 · 6 min read

120 chapters, 573,623 characters, written to the last page. I didn’t get there with better prompts — I got there with a pipeline.

The result

A modern-day sports novel, from chapter 1 to chapter 120. Every chapter was drafted by AI and reviewed by me. The book isn’t the interesting part. The interesting part is that finishing it forced a method out of me.

Wall one: it forgets

A context window cannot hold a book. By chapter 3 it has already lost the thread planted in chapter 1. By chapter 50, side characters start drifting and the setting contradicts itself.

The fix is dumb, but it works: don’t make it remember — make it look things up. After each chapter, compress “what happened, what changed for each character, which threads were planted” into a structured state record. Before writing the next chapter, feed back only the relevant state, instead of trying to stuff the whole book into the context.

That idea later became webnovel-writer: characters, settings and foreshadowing tracked chapter by chapter. By chapter 180 it still remembers the line you planted at the start.

Wall two: it makes a mess

Generated chapters come back with things that shouldn’t be there:

  • Metadata tags like [End of Chapter 12]
  • Progress-bar characters like ▓▓▓░░
  • Outline leftovers («Part One: …») mixed into the prose

These aren’t writing problems, they’re engineering problems. I wrote 11 cleanup scripts, each doing exactly one thing: strip chapter-end markers, strip progress-bar characters, strip outline leftovers, fix stray commas. Run them after every chapter and formatting goes from “check it by hand” to “mechanical”.

Wall three: it slips

The hardest errors to catch are in the content. My story is set in the present, but the model occasionally wrote “the year 2034” — a sense of time carried over from its training data, in direct conflict with the setting. So I wrote remove_years.py: find \d{4}年, delete it.

Once, a script that stripped chapter-end markers left a dangling comma behind. Without a re-read, the reader would have seen a broken sentence.

Lesson: every automatic edit needs a check behind it. Re-read after reformatting, read again after deleting characters. Automation doesn’t absolve you — it moves the checkpoint.

What I learned

AI writing isn’t one-click generation. It’s a pipeline: generation is one stage; the rest is context management, format cleanup and quality review. Once each stage became a tool, “finish a long novel” turned from luck into a process.

That’s also why all my tools are local-first: progress, character state and foreshadowing are the most private things a writer has. They shouldn’t leave your machine.

The tools

  • webnovel-writer — context and memory for long-form writing
  • inkos — a writing agent that drafts, audits and revises

The cleanup scripts are a few dozen lines each and live on my own machine. I’ll publish them as I tidy them up.
